Abstract

The purpose of this research project is to investigate the ‘overdose effect’ of AI-assisted writing. Specifically, how increasing reliance on generative AI tools impacts individual writing abilities, critical thinking, and creativity. This was done with a human study where we had participants write a short essay with varied use of AI access. One group was the control group that had no access to AI, one group had limited access, and the last had unlimited access to AI. We have collected all of the essays from all of the participants, as well as recorded all of the participants' conversations with the AI. The main result of our study is still being conducted, as we are nearly finished with the interview portion of our study, and will soon conduct the evaluation of our study.

Introduction / Background / Motivation

What did you try to do? What problem did you try to solve? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon.

We wanted to understand how using AI writing tools affects people’s ability to think, write, and create/form ideas. Our goal was to see whether students depend on AI too much helps or hurts creativity and writing skills.

How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?

Most studies today focus on how well AI can write, not on what happens to students when they use it. Few studies test the long-term effects of AI assistance on human learning and originality.

Who cares? If you are successful, what difference will it make?

Students, educators, and professionals rely on AI writing tools. Better understanding the overdose effect can help design better guidelines for using AI in a collegiate and professional environment.

Approach

What did you do exactly? How did you solve the problem? Why did you think it would be successful? Is anything new in your approach?

We ran a personalized human study where participants wrote essays under three conditions: no AI, limited AI, and unlimited AI access. We will have professional English PhD students analyze the essays based off of a standard writing rubric, with an additional question about the participants predicted AI usage (if they detect any AI and to what level). Our job is to collect the writing data from all of the users’ essays in GoogleDocs with timestamps of their AI chat logs to measure differences in writing quality, creativity, and reasoning. This includes whether or not the participant used the AI given access, what kinds of questions the participants asked, and what/how many suggestions the participant accepted. We predict that limited AI support would enhance structure and clarity, but unlimited use may reduce original writing. We changed our model for the unlimited group to encompass a more flexible form, where students could ask the AI questions that would throw errors with the limited AI platform.

What problems did you anticipate? What problems did you encounter? Did the very first thing you tried work?

We anticipated that we could only have a certain number of API calls for our LLM for a given day. We encountered problems with our unlimited AI interface with API calls to Gemini-2.5-Flash, as it is harder to implement our own LLM and have accurate responses after any given prompt. The first part of the study did not work as expected, as we implemented the interface with the same instructions for the LLM within the limited and unlimited groups. The instructions for the Google Gemini 2.5-Flash model was to give brief writing suggestions under 100 words and give writing suggestions only (nothing the user could copy and paste into the output). Since our interface was lacking in the quality of its responses, limited and unlimited participants had very similar outputs. The post interaction survey also showed similar results; the participants in the unlimited and limited group found the AI to be unhelpful in its responses.

Conclustion and Future Work

How easily are your results able to be reproduced by others? Did your dataset or annotation affect other people's choice of research or development projects to undertake? Does your work have potential harm or risk to our society? What kinds? If so, how can you address them? What limitations does your model have? How can you extend your work for future research?

Our study is able to be reproduced and replicated by others, as it is a human-conducted study that we created an interface for and launched to a group of students. No, our dataset did not affect other people’s choice of research or development projects to undertake. Our work does have potential harm if there is a misinterpretation of our findings, which could encourage the misuse or altogether elimination of AI tools in a school environment. To address this, we will emphasize the balance, acknowledgement, and responsibility of AI use, especially in an academic setting. A limitation of our model is that it only involves a small sample and a short writing task for the students to complete. Future research could extend beyond this, into long-term effects, varied writing contexts, and how AI shapes student learning over a longer period of time.
